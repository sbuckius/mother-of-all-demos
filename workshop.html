The Source
Seventeen Women, One County

Everything in this workshop starts from patents filed by women who lived in Santa Cruz, Watsonville, and the towns around them between 1903 and 1937. A steam cooker by Bertha Leibbrandt. Doll's eyes by Mabel P. Smith. A stocking drying device by Elizabeth M. Limerick. A holder for thread, cord, tape, etc. by Mildred N. Anderson. A tooth cleanser by Kate M. Remington.

This workshop will share these inventive ideas with families!

About the Workshop
Real Patents by Women, Playful Imaginative Inventions

Invent It is a two hour drop in invention workshop open to any age, built out of the research behind the performance The Mother of All Demos. Patents by women from Santa Cruz County are printed at 8.5 by 11, dozens of copies of each, stacked on the tables and pinned up on a wall. Anyone who walks in picks one and uses it as the launch pad for an invention of their own. Come with kids, come with friends, come alone.

The patents are the seed, not the assignment. A patent for a fruit dryer becomes a machine that dries feelings. A patent for a clothesline becomes a system for hanging up an entire bad mood. Six year olds, teenagers, parents, and grandparents work at the same tables, tracing, cutting, collaging, taping, and naming, and every invention is welcome no matter how impossible, expensive, or ridiculous it would be to actually build.

Because it runs as a drop in, there is no start time to miss and nothing to sign up for. You can move through the whole thing in twenty minutes or stay the full two hours and file four inventions. The workshop runs in a Yes And format. Every invention that gets filed goes up on the Chain Wall carrying a new prompt written by the person who made it, so the next person can pick it up and add to it. One historic patent branches into a chain of inventions by people who never met, and the wall gets stranger as the afternoon goes on.

How It Works
Seven Stations, Any Order, Any Time

People move through the room at their own speed and leave whenever they are done. Every invention filed leaves behind an open prompt for somebody else.

01
The Patent Wall · 5 minutes
Meet the Inventors
Patents by women from Santa Cruz County are provided in printed form for anyone to take, with the inventor's name, the year, and the town. I will guide them in the process. There is a thick stack of copies of every patent, so nobody has to share and nobody has to be careful with them. Take as many as you want back to a table.
02
The Deck · 2 minutes
Start a Chain or Join One
Two ways in. Draw a Pink Sky, Yes AND prompt card from the deck at your table and start something new from a historic patent, or walk to the Chain Wall and pull an open Yes And card that somebody already filed. Starting fresh and building on a stranger are equally good. The cards decide what your invention has to do.
03
The Tables · 15 to 20 minutes
Build It
Trace the parts you like off the patent copies, cut them out, and collage them into a machine that never existed. Draw the rest by hand on a large patent form.
04
The Patent Office · 5 minutes
File It
Inventions get an official name, a number, and a one sentence claim beginning "What I claim as my invention is..." The form is a carbonless duplicate, so everything you write lands on a second copy underneath. You sign it, the Examiner asks you one question, and it gets a serial number and an embossed seal. If you built on someone else's invention, their name goes on your form too, right under yours, because that is the whole argument of this workshop. The top sheet stays on the Chain Wall. The copy goes home with you.
05
The Patent Office · 2 minutes
Pass It On
Before you leave the desk, you write one Yes And card for someone else: "Yes, and now make it ______." The card gets added to the card deck Then you sign the register.
06
The Chain Wall · any time
Yes And
The second wall, and the one that changes all afternoon. Filed inventions hang in branching chains, each one connected by pink tape to the invention it came from and to the historic patent at the root. Every open Yes And card is an invitation. Take one, and your invention hangs off theirs. You are allowed to Yes And your own invention, and some people spend the whole two hours on one chain.
07
Every 20 minutes on the clock
The Reading
Six times across the two hours, a bell rings and one chain gets read aloud at the little pink desk from the performance. It starts with the woman whose patent began it, her name, her town, and her patent read out in full, then each inventor in order reads their own claim, sixty seconds each. Reading aloud is optional, applause is mandatory, and questions from kids are usually the hardest ones.
The Format
Yes And, All Afternoon
Yes

You accept what the last inventor made. You do not fix it, improve it, or explain why it would not work. Whatever they drew is now true.

And

You add. A part, a power, a problem it now solves, a job it now refuses. The chain only moves forward.

Then You Hand It Off

You write the next prompt before you go. Nobody leaves the patent office without leaving an invitation behind.

Everybody's Name Stays On

Each form carries the name of the person before you and the woman whose patent started the chain. Credit accumulates instead of transferring, which is the argument the whole workshop is making.

One Invention, Two Copies
Primary Method
Carbonless Duplicate Forms

The patent form is printed as a 2 or 3 part NCR set, the same paper a permit counter uses. Drawing on the top sheet presses everything through to the copy underneath. The original stays on the Chain Wall, the copy goes home, and the split is the bureaucratic gesture the whole workshop is making fun of. A third part gives me a file copy of every chain that ever ran.

Print shop · ordered ahead
Backup
Trace Your Own Drawing

The tracing paper is already on the table and they are already tracing. Two more minutes and they have made their invention twice, which is arguably the better souvenir.

Free · already in the kit
Backup
Instant Camera

A photo of the inventor holding the invention, stapled to the certificate. Kids treat a physical photo as more valuable than the drawing, and it captures the model, which no copy can.

About a dollar a shot
The Inversion
Take the Card Instead

The drawing stays because it belongs to the chain now. What goes home is the certificate and the Yes And card that was handed to them, in a stranger's handwriting. That card is proof somebody they never met asked them for something.

Free · and the strangest keepsake
Approving the Invention
The Number
Sequential Numbering Stamp

A self inking stamp that advances on its own, so every invention gets a real serial number and no two match. The click is the sound of the workshop, and by the second hour people are lining up for it.

About twenty dollars
The Seal
Hand Embosser

The notary kind, with a custom die reading CAREtech Patent Office, Pink Sky Division. It leaves a raised seal you can feel with a thumb, it works through the duplicate copy as well as the original, and it is the most convincing object in the room. Expect everything in the building to get embossed.

About thirty dollars for the die
The Role
The Examiner

Somebody other than me approves it. Whoever filed last puts on the Examiner badge, sits at the desk for ten minutes, hears the next claim read aloud, asks one question, and stamps. Approval becomes theater instead of a craft step, and it puts a child in the seat that historically told these women no.

Free · and the point
The Ledger
The Register

One oversized book on the desk. Every inventor signs it under their serial number, next to the name of the person whose invention they built on. At the end of the day the register is the record of the whole afternoon, and it opens the next session already full.

One good book · reused every time
The Deck
Pink Sky, Yes AND Prompt Cards

The starter deck, for anyone beginning a new chain from a historic patent. Each card asks you to bend that patent somewhere it was never meant to go. Once your invention is filed, you write the next card yourself.

From the Patents Themselves

These cards name the woman on the patent, so her name travels with the invention instead of stopping at the wall. Each one is handed out with a copy of the patent it comes from.

Mildred N. Anderson patented a holder for thread, cord, tape, etc. Invent the etc.
Anderson's holder keeps things from tangling. Build one for something that tangles but is not a thing.
Mabel P. Smith made eyes for a face that did not have any. Give your machine eyes, then decide what it is allowed to look at.
Elizabeth M. Limerick spent a year getting a patent for wet socks. Take the smallest annoyance in your house that seriously.
Bertha Leibbrandt patented three things in a row. Invent yours, then invent the one that comes after it.
Leibbrandt's cooker ran on steam. Rebuild your invention so it runs on steam and nothing else.
Emily and Edith Pawla patented a way to move a picture from one surface onto another. Move something off the patent in your hand and onto your invention.
The Pawlas filed together. Find someone at your table and put both names on it.
Kate M. Remington patented a tooth cleanser. Take the most boring object you can see right now and make it the invention.
Most of these patents are for work done at home. Invent the machine for the housework nobody has named yet.
Source Material
Santa Cruz Patents by Women

The workshop draws on the patent research behind The Mother of All Demos, narrowed to inventors from Santa Cruz County so the history sits in the same place the people in the room live. Seventeen patents, filed by women of Santa Cruz, Watsonville, and the towns around them between 1903 and 1937. Ten to fifteen come to each session, printed at 8.5 by 11 with dozens of copies of each, so they can be traced over, cut apart, and collaged without anyone worrying about ruining the only one.

1926 · Number to confirm
Mildred N. Anderson · Santa Cruz
Holder for Thread, Cord, Tape, Etc. A thing that keeps other things from getting tangled. The "etc." is doing a lot of quiet work.
What else belongs in the etc.?
1919 · Number to confirm
Mabel P. Smith · Santa Cruz
Doll's Eyes. Eyes made to be put into a face that does not have any yet.
What should your machine be able to see?

Stocking-Drying Device. Filed in February, granted the next January, for the problem of wet socks.

Steam-Cooker. She also patented a way of making bread from whole grain and a way of treating grain for beverages. Three ideas, one after another.

Tooth Cleanser. The most recent patent in the collection, and the least glamorous.
What boring thing could be a great invention?

For Host Venues
What I Need, What I Bring
Workshop Requirements ▼

Space

  • Room for 5 to 6 tables that seat 4 people each
  • Two walls, boards, or hanging systems: one for the historic patents, one for the Chain Wall where filed inventions branch out during the session
  • Wall space for roughly 25 sheets at 8.5 x 11 in to start, growing to about 80 by the end. Chains branch sideways, so wide beats tall
  • Floor space for the pink reading desk, about 6 ft by 8 ft, visible from the tables
  • Sink or water access nearby, glue and small hands

From the Venue

  • Tables and chairs, including a few low chairs for small kids
  • Table covering if the surfaces need protecting
  • Recycling and trash bins, emptied once mid session
  • One staff member or volunteer at the door for the full two hours, greeting arrivals and keeping the room at about 20 people at a time
  • Optional microphone if the room is large or echoey

Timing

  • Two hours, open the whole time, no fixed start
  • 60 minutes of setup before, 45 minutes of strike after
  • Readings run every 20 minutes, six across the session
  • Can extend to three hours with additional materials

Items That I Bring

  • Patent copies at 8.5 x 11, dozens of each, in labeled stacks
  • Pink Sky, Yes AND prompt card decks, one per table
  • Carbonless duplicate patent forms and certificates for 80 people
  • Sequential numbering stamp, hand embosser, and ink
  • Examiner badges and the register book
  • Instant camera and film
  • Tracing paper, scissors, glue sticks, and the rest of the drawing and building materials
  • Pink desk, pink baskets, worker badges, and the stamp
  • Pink gaffer tape
  • Yes And cards, clips, and pink tape for the chains
  • A bell
Connected Work
Where This Comes From

The workshop grew out of The Mother of All Demos, a participatory multimedia performance that memorializes 504 patents by women, reads Matilda Joslyn Gage's 1870 article "Woman as Inventor" aloud, and pays its audience for their labor. The performance argues that invention by women has always been happening and has always been credited elsewhere, a pattern named the Matilda Effect.

The performance makes that argument to an audience. The workshop hands the argument to whoever walks in and asks them to do something about it, at a table, with tape.
